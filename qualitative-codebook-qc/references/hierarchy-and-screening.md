# Hierarchy and screening

## Levels

Three levels, named consistently:

- **Grandparent** - the top-level domain. Usually 4-8. This is the level that
  appears in a summary count, so it must not mix constructs.
- **Parent** - a category within a domain. Usually 12-20 across the whole
  framework.
- **Grandchild** - the existing leaf code. Do not rewrite it. It is the
  participant's or coder's own label and the reader needs to see it unchanged.

Code them `<number> <name>` for grandparents and `<number>.<number> <name>` for
parents (`3 Caregiver stress, coping and self-care`, `3.1 Emotional strain and
stress disclosure`). The dotted prefix lets any tool check nesting mechanically,
which matters more than it sounds: it is the cheapest way to catch a
miscategorised row.

## Designing the levels

- **Meaning beats symmetry.** A parent holding one member is fine if it carries a
  distinct idea. Merging it into a neighbour to make the table look even destroys
  the distinction the reader needs.
- **Name the level for the question, not the content.** "Constraints on transfer"
  tells a reader what the stratum does. "Barriers" does not.
- **Do not create a level to hold the leftovers.** If items do not fit, either
  the rule is wrong or they belong in the excluded log with a reason.
- **Splitting a construct is the point.** When a theme turns out to contain two
  different things - what people did, and what they said about doing it - give
  each its own grandparent. Never leave them pooled because they arrived
  together.

## The screening rule

Freeze it before coding. Three parts, all three written into the deliverable:

1. **Inclusion rule** - the positive test, in one sentence. Example: *the item
   describes using, attempting to use, intending to use or failing to use the
   training content in the participant's own life, or names a condition that
   shapes such use.*
2. **Exclusion rule** - the categories you will not code, named. Example:
   *content description, delivery or facilitator view, satisfaction rating,
   programme-design or cost preference, care logistics, condition-of-the-patient
   status.* Naming them stops drift later.
3. **Boundary rule** - the distinction most likely to be blurred, stated as a
   rule. Example: *reflective engagement with the intervention is coded but never
   pooled with enacted practice.*

State the rule before assigning codes. A framework assembled first and justified
afterwards cannot be audited: the reader cannot tell which items were never
considered, only which ones did not make it.

## Three-way screening

Every source item gets exactly one decision and a written reason:

| Decision | Meaning | Appears in the coded sheet? |
|---|---|---|
| **Include** | Meets the inclusion rule | Yes |
| **Borderline** | Plausible, but the quotation does not carry the required evidence | No - logged so the reviewer can promote it |
| **Exclude** | Falls under a named exclusion | No |

Borderline is not indecision. It is a **reviewer queue**: the calls a second
coder is most likely to contest, surfaced rather than hidden inside a judgement
call. A run that returns no borderline items has usually been over-confident.

Keep the reasons short and specific. `"CR health status; no reference to using
the training."` beats `"not relevant"`. Where two items share a reason, say so
explicitly (`"As code 10 - ..."`) rather than restating it, so a reader can see
they are the same call.

## The disconfirming case

Before finalising, search deliberately for the item that contradicts the theme:

- the participant who says the content **does not apply**;
- the change that happened **without** the intervention;
- the item that shows the framework's boundary is wrong.

Give these their own stratum or parent. A theme that reports only confirming
evidence has discarded its most useful output, and a reviewer will ask for
exactly this.

## Framing the analytic question

If the deliverable states a question, it must be answerable by the coding and
free of presupposition.

- Begin with **how** or **what**. "Why" implies cause and belongs elsewhere.
- Delete directional verbs: *affect, influence, impact, determine, cause, relate*.
- Delete words that presuppose the finding.
- One central question. Subquestions only if the framework maps onto them.

Example: *How do family caregivers of people with dementia describe using, or
not using, ACT skills in everyday caregiving?* Note the "or not using" - the
disconfirming case is built into the question, not bolted on afterwards.

## Unit of analysis

The unit is the existing leaf code plus its quotation, and it is assigned once.
When one quotation contains two ideas - as long quotations often do - assign it
to the dominant one and record the other in a QC note pointing at the code that
does carry it. Silently assigning it twice inflates every count downstream.
