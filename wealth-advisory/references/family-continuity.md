# Family continuity and protection

For a household with dependent children, this file matters more than the investment section. A portfolio problem costs money; a protection problem costs the plan.

## Work the layers in order

### 1. Income stops - death of a breadwinner

Size the cover against what the family would actually need, not a rule of thumb:

- Income replacement until the youngest child is independent
- Outstanding debt, including the mortgage
- Education costs still to come
- A margin for the survivor's changed working pattern
- **Minus** existing cover, CPF savings available, and any survivor benefits

In a household where one spouse is a Singapore citizen and the other a permanent resident, the CPF position differs between them. Do not assume one calculation covers both lives; run it separately for each.

### 2. Income stops but the person lives - disability

This is the most under-insured risk in most families and the one that damages savings fastest, because it removes income and creates costs at the same time. Ask what the family would live on if a breadwinner could not work for three years. If the honest answer is "savings", the savings are the disability policy.

### 3. Serious illness

Critical illness cover and adequate hospitalisation cover. Check what the Integrated Shield plan actually covers, including the deductible and co-insurance the household would pay before any payout.

### 4. Long-term care

CareShield Life provides a basic income that is usually well short of the real cost of care. Quantify the monthly gap against actual nursing or home-care costs and test what funding that gap does to the portfolio.

### 5. Emergency fund

Size it in months of household spending, not income. Two incomes and stable employment might justify six months; one income, a young family, or a planned relocation justifies twelve months or more. It belongs in cash and near-cash, not in the portfolio.

Only after these five does portfolio risk become the main question.

## Paperwork that protects the family

- **CPF nominations.** Without a nomination, CPF savings are distributed by the Public Trustee under the intestacy rules. That is slower, public in its process, and may not match what the household intends. Both spouses should have valid nominations, kept current after any life change.
- **SRS nominations.** Separate from the CPF nomination; easy to forget.
- **A will.** Essential whenever there are minor children. It is how a guardian gets named. Without one, the court decides, and a two-country family is exactly the case where that goes wrong.
- **Lasting Power of Attorney.** For a household that will live across two jurisdictions, this is not optional paperwork. Both spouses.
- **Insurance nominations** and, where relevant, trust arrangements.
- **A continuity file.** One document that says where the accounts are, who the advisers are, what cover exists, and what the plan is. If both parents are unavailable, a relative should be able to act from that file alone. Most families have this knowledge spread across two people's heads and nowhere else.

Anything involving wills, trusts, guardianship, or citizenship has consequences that do not reverse. Take it to a lawyer who works with cross-border families rather than drafting it from a template.

## This household's specific wrinkles

The family described in this plan has a mixed immigration profile - a Singapore-citizen spouse and children, and a Chinese-citizen permanent resident - plus a planned relocation to China. That combination creates planning points a single-country tool would miss entirely:

- **National Service for the son.** Mandatory for male Singapore citizens, and it cannot be avoided by relocating. It also anchors the family in Singapore until he has served, which in practice sets the earliest realistic relocation date. Treat that date as a constraint on the plan, not as something to optimise around.
- **Two different CPF positions.** The citizen spouse's CPF is permanent and CPF LIFE continues wherever she lives. The PR spouse's CPF is contingent on holding permanent residency with a valid re-entry permit. If PR is given up on departure, CPF becomes a lump sum to manage rather than a lifetime income. This single decision determines whether the household has one lifetime income floor or one and a half.
- **Re-entry permit renewal** while living abroad. Letting it lapse is a decision with consequences, and losing PR status changes both the CPF treatment and the option to return.
- **The children's citizenship** is a future decision with irrevocable elements, especially given the son's service obligation. Specialist territory, and not something to solve with a calculator.
- **Healthcare after the move.** MediShield Life and Integrated Shield cover is built for treatment in Singapore and is not a substitute for cover in China. Budget for separate health cover as a real line item, not an afterthought.
- **Tax residency shift.** Singapore and China tax residents differently, and a Singapore-China double tax agreement applies. Confirm the position for both spouses before the move, and note that the citizen spouse's tax status may differ from the PR spouse's.

## Education funding

Both children are likely to attend local universities, which removes the overseas-tuition scenario that derails most family plans. Two things still need care:

- **Fees depend on citizenship category.** Citizen, permanent resident, and international student rates differ materially. Since the children are citizens, they should qualify for the lowest tier, but confirm current fee levels rather than assuming.
- **Timing collides with the relocation.** Fees fall due in the years immediately before the move, when the portfolio is being transitioned to another currency. That is precisely the money that should be held in Singapore dollars, in low-risk assets, with no currency exposure.

## Household coordination

Two working spouses usually means two sets of reliefs and two sets of accounts. Check the household position rather than the individual one:

- SRS contributions for both spouses, each against their own cap
- CPF cash top-ups, including to a spouse, where the relief is available
- Which spouse's account should be drawn first in retirement, given that the tax and CPF treatment differs between them
- Nominations on every account, in both names

## Review cadence

Once a year, plus after any of these: a birth or death in the family, a change of employment, a property purchase or sale, a change in immigration status, a relocation date change, or any change to the law that affects CPF, SRS, or tax residency.
