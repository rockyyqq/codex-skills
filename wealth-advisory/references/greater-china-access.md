# Greater China: Hong Kong and mainland access

Read this before treating "Hong Kong" and "China" as two separate allocations.

## They are usually one exposure

Hong Kong is mostly a *wrapper* for mainland exposure, not a separate bet. H-shares, red chips, P-chips, China A-share ETFs listed in Hong Kong, and Hong Kong-listed technology companies that earn their revenue on the mainland all end up in the same economic pile.

A household holding "15% Hong Kong plus 10% China" through several vehicles may be far more concentrated than it believes. Always measure regional exposure **look-through** - what the underlying businesses actually are - not by where the fund is listed.

## What you actually own, by route

| Route | What you hold | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Hong Kong-listed local companies | The share itself | Little; the most direct ownership here |
| H-shares (mainland companies listed in HK) | The share itself | Chinese dividend withholding; capital gains treatment differs from A-shares |
| Red chips / P-chips | Shares in an offshore holding company that owns mainland businesses | You own the offshore company, not the operating business |
| VIE structures - most offshore China tech | A contractual claim, not ownership | If the arrangement is challenged, the claim may not be enforceable. This risk is invisible in a volatility figure |
| A-shares via Stock Connect (Northbound) | The A-share, held through Hong Kong clearing | Investor eligibility rules apply, and there are quota and trading-calendar constraints |
| Hong Kong-listed ETF | Fund units | The underlying may be A-shares, H-shares, or derivatives. Check whether it is physical or synthetic |
| US-listed ADR or ETF | The security, US-situs for tax | US estate tax exposure above USD 60,000, and US dividend withholding |
| Irish-domiciled UCITS ETF | Fund units, non-US situs | Usually no US estate exposure; check the withholding on the underlying dividends |

## Tax by route

Confirm every line before relying on it - these change, and several have temporary measures attached.

| Where held | Dividends | Capital gains | Dealing cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hong Kong-listed, Hong Kong tax | No Hong Kong withholding | No Hong Kong capital gains tax | 0.1% stamp duty on each side, plus brokerage **(confirmed)** |
| H-shares | Chinese withholding on dividends | Generally not taxed in China for non-residents | HK stamp duty |
| A-shares via Stock Connect | Chinese withholding on dividends | Temporarily exempt for foreign investors - a temporary measure that has been extended repeatedly | Verify which stamp duties apply |
| US-listed ADR or US-domiciled ETF | 30% US withholding for a Singapore resident, as there is no US-Singapore tax treaty | No US capital gains tax for non-residents | US estate tax exposure above USD 60,000 **(confirmed - IRS)** |
| Irish-domiciled UCITS ETF | Often 15% US withholding on US-sourced dividends | No US capital gains tax | No US estate tax exposure |

## Domicile is the quietest and largest decision

For a Singapore-resident household that is not US-connected, two funds tracking the same index can differ substantially depending on where the fund is domiciled:

- **US-domiciled** holdings are US-situated assets. Above USD 60,000, they expose the estate to US estate tax, with a filing obligation on Form 706-NA. The IRS is explicit that US-incorporated company stock counts even when the certificate is held abroad. **(confirmed - IRS)**
- **Irish-domiciled UCITS** holdings are not US-situated, and typically enjoy a lower rate of US dividend withholding.

Same exposure, materially different tax outcome. Nothing about the fund's name reveals this, which is why the portfolio tool asks for domicile as a field.

## Currency: the part that matters most here

- The Hong Kong dollar is pegged to the US dollar. Holding Hong Kong assets therefore carries **US dollar** risk, not renminbi risk.
- A-shares and onshore assets are renminbi; offshore renminbi (CNH) trades more freely than onshore CNY.

This has a direct consequence for a household that will eventually spend in renminbi: **buying Hong Kong shares does not hedge a future renminbi spending need.** It swaps renminbi exposure for dollar exposure. Only renminbi-denominated assets move with the liability. Treating Hong Kong as "China exposure" for currency purposes is a common and expensive mistake.

## Risks specific to this region

- **Policy and regulatory intervention.** Rules can change quickly and retrospectively; a sector can be repriced by an administrative decision rather than by earnings.
- **VIE enforceability.** Much of the offshore technology sector is a contract, not a share.
- **Listing and audit access.** Offshore listings have faced delisting risk over audit inspection access.
- **Capital controls and repatriation.** Money does not always leave as freely as it arrived.
- **Governance.** State ownership, related-party dealings, and disclosure quality vary widely.
- **Index concentration.** A small number of companies and sectors dominate the headline indices, so a broad index may be less diversified than it looks.
- **Price limits and suspensions.** A-share daily limits and trading halts can trap you at exactly the moment you want to sell.
- **Illiquidity in smaller Hong Kong stocks**, where spreads widen sharply in stress.
- **Investment restrictions** that can apply to certain persons and entities.

## Due diligence on any Greater China vehicle

1. What am I actually buying - a share, a fund unit, a derivative, or a contractual claim?
2. Where is the fund domiciled, and what does that mean for estate tax?
3. What index does it track, and how concentrated is that index really?
4. Is it physical or synthetic, and who is the counterparty?
5. What is the all-in cost, including stamp duty, FX conversion, and platform fees?
6. What withholding applies to the dividends, and at what rate?
7. Can I repatriate, and how long does it take?
8. What happens to my position in a suspension or a market-wide closure?

## Verification status

Confirmed against official sources on 10 September 2026:

- Hong Kong stamp duty of 0.1% on each side of a Hong Kong stock transfer, effective 17 November 2023 - `https://www.gov.hk/en/residents/taxes/stamp/stamp_duty_rates.htm`
- US estate tax exposure for non-residents on US-situated assets above USD 60,000, including US-incorporated company stock - `https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/some-nonresidents-with-us-assets-must-file-estate-tax-returns`

- Stock Connect Northbound eligibility for an individual investor resident in Singapore
- US dividend withholding rates for Singapore residents, and the rate applied to Irish-domiciled funds
- Which stamp duties apply to Northbound Stock Connect trades
