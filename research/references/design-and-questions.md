# Design and questions

Source: Creswell, *Research Design: Qualitative, Quantitative, and Mixed Methods
Approaches* (2nd ed., 2003). The design decisions below are unchanged in later
editions, but the naming has moved on: the 3rd and 4th editions replace the
concurrent/sequential/transformative typology with convergent parallel,
explanatory sequential, exploratory sequential and embedded designs, and use the
QUAN/qual notation more systematically. If a protocol will be read by a methods
reviewer, cite the edition you followed.

`search-and-screening.md` frames a question for a **search**. This file frames a
question for a **study**, which is a different job: it has to match the approach,
survive a committee, and be answerable with the data that can be collected.

## Match the question to the approach

- **Quantitative** - the variables and the theory are known, and the question
  asks how much, how often, or whether one thing predicts another. Testing a
  theory; hypotheses fixed in advance.
- **Qualitative** - the concept is immature, the available theory may be
  inaccurate or biased, or the point is to understand meaning and process. The
  question is deliberately broad, and it is expected to evolve during the study
  as the data teach you what to ask.
- **Mixed methods** - one approach cannot answer the question alone: a
  quantitative result needs explaining, an unknown construct needs measuring, or
  a finding needs testing in the population it came from.

Choosing an approach because it is the one you know how to do is the failure
this decision exists to prevent. Write down the question first, then the
approach it forces.

## Quantitative questions and hypotheses

A useful sequence is **descriptive questions first, inferential second**. The
descriptive questions name the variables, the population and the setting; the
inferential questions test the relationships or group differences, controlling
for the covariates you named. A reader should be able to see which variable is
independent, which is dependent, and which are being held constant - before the
Methods section.

Hypotheses add:

- the theory or prior finding they are derived from, so the prediction is not
  arbitrary;
- the **null** and **alternative** forms, stated so that a reader could tell
  which analyses test them;
- **directional** or **non-directional**, chosen deliberately. A directional
  hypothesis is a stronger claim and needs a reason to expect that direction.
- the primary hypothesis labelled as primary, since secondary and exploratory
  analyses will be reported as such.

## Qualitative questions

- **One or two central questions**, with no more than five to seven
  subquestions for each. A dozen questions in total is the practical ceiling;
  beyond that the study has no centre.
- Begin with **what** or **how**. "Why" implies cause and effect and belongs to
  a different approach.
- Delete directional verbs - *affect*, *influence*, *impact*, *determine*,
  *cause*, *relate* - along with words that presuppose a finding.
- Use the exploratory verb that matches the strategy: *discover* (grounded
  theory), *seek to understand* (ethnography), *explore a process* (case study),
  *describe the experiences* (phenomenology), *report the stories* (narrative).
- Keep the questions open-ended and largely free of the literature's typologies,
  unless the specific strategy calls for building on existing theory.
- Say that the questions may evolve. In a qualitative design that is a method,
  not an admission of vagueness - and where they did change, report how and why.

## Purpose statement scripts

Creswell's scripts exist so that the purpose statement cannot omit a component:
strategy, central phenomenon, participants, site, and how the construct is
understood at this stage.

**Qualitative:**

> The purpose of this [strategy of inquiry] study is to [understand / describe /
> develop / discover] [central phenomenon] for [participants] at [research site].
> At this stage in the research, [central phenomenon] will be generally defined
> as [general definition].

**Quantitative:**

> The purpose of this [experiment / survey] study is to test the theory of
> [theory] that [relates / compares] [independent variable] to [dependent
> variable], controlling for [control variables], for [participants] at [research
> site]. [Variables] will be generally defined as [general definition], and the
> control and intervening variables will be statistically controlled.

**Mixed methods**, in two phases, naming the sequence and the priority:

> The purpose of this two-phase, [sequential / concurrent] mixed methods study
> is to [first purpose] and then to [second purpose]. In the first phase,
> [quantitative or qualitative data] will be collected and analysed to [aim]; the
> second phase will [build on / explain / test] those results.

The statement is one paragraph and it commits. Whatever is not in it will not be
in the protocol.

## The introduction: the deficiencies model

Five components, in order, each doing one job:

1. **The problem.** The issue or concern that motivates the study, stated in the
   first paragraph - not the topic, and not the question.
2. **The literature about the problem.** What is known, selected rather than
   exhaustive, organised by idea rather than by author.
3. **The deficiencies in that literature.** What is missing, contradictory or
   inadequate: the population never studied, the measure never validated in this
   language, the mechanism assumed but untested, the trial never done against a
   real comparator. This is the paragraph that justifies the study.
4. **The audience.** Who needs the answer, and why it matters to them. A gap that
   matters to nobody is not a gap.
5. **The purpose.** The statement above.

The order is the argument: a reader who stops after the introduction should know
the problem, the gap, who cares and what you will do about it. Most introductions
that are rejected for "unclear significance" have a component missing, usually
the deficiencies paragraph - they describe a topic thoroughly and never say what
is wrong with the literature they just summarised.

## Where the theory goes

- **Quantitative:** at the front, before the hypotheses, as the thing being
  tested. The theory is a prediction machine, and the discussion returns to it.
- **Qualitative:** often at the end, emerging from the data (grounded theory), or
  at the front as an explicit lens. Either is defensible; not saying which is
  not.
- **Mixed methods:** state whether the theoretical perspective is explicit
  (gender, ethnicity, class, a health-services framework) or implicit. If it is
  explicit, say where it operates: the questions, the sampling, the instrument,
  the interpretation.

## Sampling

| Approach | Logic | What the write-up must say |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative | Probability sampling from a defined list, so the sample can support estimates about the population | Population and sampling frame, stratification, how the number was computed (formula or power analysis), the percentage approached who participated, and the self-selection that remains |
| Qualitative | Purposive sampling of information-rich cases | The strategy (typical, extreme, maximal variation, snowball, criterion), who was chosen and why, what the sample could and could not represent, and how saturation was judged |
| Mixed methods | Both of the above, deliberately related | Both sets of sampling decisions, and how the strands relate - nested, parallel, or one sampled from the other |

For an experiment, the sample size comes from a power analysis: alpha (convention
.05), power (convention .80), and the effect size expressed in standard deviation
units (.50 in Creswell's worked example). Name all three inputs and their source.
A power calculation built on an effect size from a pilot is a calculation built
on an inflated number - see `research-progression.md`. For what each input has to
be defensible against, and how the answer is reported, read
`sample-size-and-power.md`.

## Mixed-methods design: four decisions before the label

1. **Implementation** - concurrent (both strands at once) or sequential (one
   informs the next), and if sequential, which comes first.
2. **Priority** - equal weight, or one dominant. This is what the notation
   records: capitals for the dominant strand (QUAN + qual), lower case for the
   supporting one.
3. **Integration** - the stage at which the strands actually meet: during data
   collection, at analysis, or at interpretation. A mixed-methods study that
   never says where the strands meet is two studies stapled together.
4. **Theoretical perspective** - explicit or implicit, and if explicit, which.

Then, and only then, name the design and give the reason it matches the question.
The recurring designs and the question each answers:

- **Convergent** - both strands collected in parallel, compared at
  interpretation, where a quantitative estimate and a qualitative account are
  meant to corroborate or to expose where they disagree.
- **Explanatory sequential** (quantitative first) - a quantitative result is
  known and the qualitative phase explains it: why uptake was low, why the
  effect appeared in one subgroup.
- **Exploratory sequential** (qualitative first) - the construct, instrument or
  intervention is not yet well specified, so the qualitative phase builds it and
  the quantitative phase tests it.
- **Embedded** - one strand sits inside a design built for the other, as a
  process evaluation inside a trial.

A mixed-methods procedures section has to state: the design and why; a visual
model of the strands and the points of interface; both sampling strategies;
both sets of data-collection and analysis procedures; how each strand is
validated in its own terms; how the integration is analysed rather than
asserted; and the narrative structure the report will follow.

## Validity in each strand, and across them

Quantitative validity (internal, external, construct, statistical conclusion)
and qualitative trustworthiness - Lincoln and Guba's credibility,
transferability, dependability and confirmability, with the techniques actually
used to establish each - are different vocabularies for the same duty: showing
why a reader should believe this account. State both.

The mixed-methods question is a third one: **what happens when the strands
disagree?** Plan for convergence, expansion, and non-convergence, and say which
of the three you found. Reporting only the parts that agree is the failure mode
that makes integration decorative.

Mixed methods has no reporting guideline as entrenched as CONSORT or PRISMA.
Check the EQUATOR Network for the current entry before naming one.

## Ethics, written into the method

Name the approval body and the reference number; the consent procedure and who
obtained it; how participants were protected where the researcher had power over
them (students, patients, staff); what was done with incidental findings; where
the data are stored, for how long, under which data-protection framework, and
who has access. For qualitative work add how participants will be represented,
how quotations will be de-identified where the setting makes identity obvious,
and what participants were told about how the findings would be used.

A methods section that mentions ethics only as a sentence about approval has
answered a form, not a reviewer.
