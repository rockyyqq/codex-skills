# Writing practice

Two sources, both about producing prose rather than about what the prose should
say:

- Silvia, *How to Write a Lot* (2007) - the working habits of productive
  academic writers. Opinionated rather than evidenced in places, and marked as
  such below.
- Strunk, *The Elements of Style* (1918 edition) - the composition and usage
  rules a copyeditor applies. This is the original Strunk, not Strunk and White,
  and it predates modern usage rulings; see the note at the end on what to
  discard.

`writing-style.md` describes the author's voice. This file describes how the
draft gets written and how it gets cleaned up.

## Running the writing

**Schedule, do not find.** The belief that writing needs an undiscovered block
of time ("I can't find time to write") is the most common reason a capable
researcher publishes little. Nobody finds time to teach; the teaching is
scheduled. Writing works the same way. Four scheduled hours per week is the
starting dose, and **regularity matters more than the number of hours**.

**Binge writing is the failure mode, and it is self-reinforcing.** Intend,
procrastinate, feel guilty, write all Saturday, feel better, repeat. The guilt
and the anxiety consume more hours than the writing the schedule would have
produced. The related traps are binge reading and binge analysis: the
preparation for writing - literature, analyses, figures, proofs, forms - is
done in the scheduled slot or it does not get done at all.

**Treat writing time as unavailable.** Close the network, silence the phone,
and say no to the meeting. Treat it exactly as you treat a scheduled class.
Where the request comes from someone more senior, "I can't, I have a standing
commitment" is a complete answer.

**Interrogate the barriers by name.** Silvia's four ("need more analyses first",
"need to read more first", "need a better computer/chair/room", "waiting until
I feel like it") are all false, and the equipment one is the most obviously
false: paper and a keyboard are sufficient, and an internet connection in the
writing room is a cost, not a benefit.

**The evidence on inspiration.** Boice's randomised study of struggling
academics, as reported in the book: those required to write during scheduled
sessions wrote about **3.5 times** as much as those who wrote only when
inspired, and about **16 times** as much as those who were told not to write at
all; creative ideas for writing arrived roughly every 1 day versus 2 days versus
5 days. Writing produces the ideas for writing. "Writer's block" in technical
writing is, on this view, a description of not writing rather than a cause of
it (**opinion**, clearly labelled as such by its author, and worth treating as a
useful provocation rather than a finding).

**Set three levels of goal.** A written list of **project goals** (resubmit a
paper, start a manuscript, revive the abandoned one, draft the grant), kept
visible; a **priority order** among them; and a **concrete goal for each
session** - "print yesterday's draft and revise it", "add the missing references
and reconcile the citations against the text", "draft the first three paragraphs
of the general discussion", "write at least 200 words". "Work on the paper" is
not a goal.

A workable priority order, from the book's survey of productive academics:
page proofs and copyedited manuscripts (hard deadlines, usually 48 hours);
projects with deadlines; revising a manuscript to resubmit (closer to
publication than anything new); reviewing manuscripts and grants (do it fast -
the system is only as good as its reviewers); new manuscripts; miscellaneous
writing. Projects with a graduate student co-author move up. Graduate students
ordering their own work: deadline projects, then the degree-defining writing
(thesis, comprehensive paper, dissertation), then professional publications,
then the rest.

**Monitor.** Keep a one-line-per-session log: date, project, whether the goal
was met, and words written where words were the output. Self-monitoring alone
changes behaviour, and after a few months the log is the only honest estimate
you will have of how long a paper takes you. Reward a finished project - never
by skipping the next session.

**Write in company.** An "agraphia group" is a standing meeting, weekly or
fortnightly, whose entire business is writing goals: each member states concrete
goals for the coming period, the goals are written into a shared folder, and at
the next meeting they are read back and each one is marked met or unmet. Goals
have to be specific - "make an outline for the paper" passes, "think about my
paper" and "try to write 100 words" are rejected, because trying to write is not
writing. Members reward each other for submissions, and the group confronts
someone who consistently misses goals (the diagnostic question is whether they
have a schedule at all). The meeting is not a forum for complaining about
teaching or committees - that is the way such a group dies.

**Submit a clean manuscript, and read the instructions to authors first.**
Journal submission guidelines differ, and "I'll tidy it up at revision" wastes
the one review round you were given. The cover letter is boilerplate -
manuscript title, contact details, the standard assurances - not an argument
for the paper. Editors read the paper.

**Resubmission letters are a genre with a formula.** Thank the editor; then,
under a heading for each reviewer, respond point by point in numbered items,
quoting the comment, stating what changed, and naming where it changed. Where
you disagree, say why with evidence and say what you did instead. Never argue
with the tone of a review; never leave a point unanswered. This letter is read
more closely than anything else you will write that month.

## The editing pass

**Generate and revise in separate passes.** They are different tasks with
opposite instructions. A first draft written for correctness is written one
sentence at a time, sounds disjointed, and takes five times as long. The
paragraph, not the sentence, is the unit. Draft badly and quickly, then revise.

Search for these by name, because they survive rereading by eye:

**Words to delete**

- Parasitic intensifiers: *very, quite, basically, actually, virtually,
  extremely, remarkably, completely, at all, so* (as an intensifier). They drain
  the vigour from the word they modify.
- `the fact that` and its family: *owing to the fact that* → **because**;
  *in spite of the fact that* → **although**; *the fact that he had not
  succeeded* → **his failure**; *the question as to whether* → **whether**.
- *in nature* after an adjective (redundant), *in a manner* instead of an adverb
  (*responded rapidly*), *case, character, factor, feature, system, line along
  these lines, phase* used as filler nouns.
- *individual* and *individuals* where *person* and *people* will do; *persons*
  in the legal sense only.
- *there is / there are* openings, *who is / which was* clauses, and *he is a
  man who* → *he*.
- *such that* - never. The cure is to delete the flabby clause before it, to
  replace it with a colon or dash, or to rewrite the sentence.
- Abbreviations that are not easier than the phrase: *SES* and *ANOVA* save
  work, *ANX* and *DEP* cost the reader more than they save.

**Sentences**

- **Wobbly compounds**: a comma before *and* joining a clause with no subject
  ("Positive moods enhance creative problem solving, and broaden thinking").
  Add the subject or drop the comma.
- **Verb buried in a noun**: *is indicative of* → **indicates**; *is reflective
  of* → **reflects**; *is supportive of* → **supports**; *a survey of this
  region was made* → **this region was surveyed**; *mobilization was carried
  out* → **the army was mobilized**.
- **Negative form where positive is stronger**: *did not remember* → **forgot**;
  *did not pay attention to* → **ignored**; *not honest* → **dishonest**. Keep
  *not* for genuine denial and antithesis, not as a hedge.
- **Successive loose sentences** - the paragraph of *and*-joined clauses that
  drones. Recast some as simple sentences, join others with semicolons, and keep
  the emphasis for the end of the sentence where a reader expects it.
- **Parallelism**: express coordinate ideas in similar form. Variation for its
  own sake makes a writer look unsure; the repetition is the clarity.
- **Related words together**: keep the modifying phrase next to what it
  modifies, and the relative pronoun next to its antecedent - or the sentence
  says something you did not mean.

**Punctuation and word pairs a copyeditor will flag**

- Serial comma in a list of three or more; enclose parenthetic expressions
  between commas.
- Possessive of a singular noun takes *'s* whatever the final consonant:
  *Charles's* not *Charles'* (US practice; the ancient-name exceptions are
  *Jesus'*, *Achilles'*).
- Do not join independent clauses with a comma, and do not break one sentence
  into two fragments.
- *however* meaning *nevertheless* does not open its clause; putting it first
  makes a run-on or changes the meaning to "in whatever way".
- *compare to* (different orders of things) versus *compare with* (same order);
  *due to* (as a modifier of a noun) versus *owing to / because of* (adverbial);
  *fewer* (countable) versus *less* (quantity); *most* versus *almost*; *while*
  only in the literal sense of *during the time that*; *that* (restrictive, no
  comma) versus *which* (non-restrictive, comma); *et al.* for people, *etc.*
  never for people.
- Semicolons join two independent clauses that the reader should weigh against
  each other; they are also the fix for the *however* run-on. Em dashes set off
  an inserted clause or an aside; en dashes coordinate two concepts or a range
  (*parent–child*, *2019–2024*), which a hyphen does not.

**Keep terms exact.** A concept gets one name and keeps it for the whole
manuscript. Varying the wording for elegance forces the reader to work out
whether you mean the same thing; in a results section, they cannot tell, and
will assume you do not.

**What to discard from a 1918 style book.** Strunk prescribes *he* for
distributive pronouns such as *each*, *everybody* and *anybody*. That rule is
obsolete: follow the target journal, and use singular *they* or rewrite. Take
the composition principles - concise, active, positive, concrete, parallel - and
leave the dated usage rulings, which also appear in his word lists.

**Two house-style decisions to make once per manuscript:** US or UK spelling
(pick one and apply it everywhere; a mixed manuscript reads as unedited), and
the target journal's exceptions to any rule above (the journal wins; APA, for
instance, adopts the serial comma but not every publisher does).
