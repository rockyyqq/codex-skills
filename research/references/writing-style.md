# Writing style

Derived from eight full papers spanning 2016 to 2025, about 71,600 words in
total. Sentence-length figures come from automated extraction, so headings,
tables and reference lists inflate them - treat the numbers as relative, not
exact. Where a target journal's house style conflicts with anything here, the
journal wins; say so rather than silently mixing the two.

This describes how to write *in the author's voice*. It never applies to
quotation: a quoted sentence is reproduced exactly as published.

It describes the finished voice, not how the draft gets produced. For the
working habits and the sentence-level editing pass - words to delete, sentence
faults, punctuation and word pairs - see `writing-practice.md`.

## Shape

- Structured abstracts with labelled headings: Background, Methods, Results,
  then Conclusions. Not a single narrative paragraph.
- IMRaD in the body.
- The abstract closes on an implication or a need for further work, not on a
  bare result.

## The design-first opener

The most distinctive habit in the corpus. The Discussion or Conclusion opens by
naming the design in a prepositional phrase, then stating what it found:

- "Using a repeated cross-sectional survey design, our study found a significant
  upward trend ..."
- "Through a mixed-method RCT, this study demonstrates the feasibility of ..."
- "Through a person-centered analytical approach, the current study confirmed ..."

The design and the finding arrive in the same sentence, before any
interpretation. Use this construction rather than opening a discussion with
background or with a claim about the literature.

## Self-reference

The work is named rather than left implicit. "The current study" appears about 61
times across the corpus, and "this study" or "our study" about 91 times -
together roughly 2.1 per 1,000 words. The usual verbs are *explored*, *examined*,
*confirmed*, *tested* and *found*.

## Sentence rhythm

The typical sentence is short to medium - a median of about 18 to 21 words - with
a long tail reaching roughly 40 words at the 90th percentile. The pattern is many
plain declaratives punctuated by the occasional long cumulative sentence, rather
than uniformly complex prose.

Punctuation carries the load: semicolons appear about 6 times per 1,000 words,
and comma-separated lists with a serial comma about 5.5 times. Parallel clauses
are joined with semicolons rather than split into separate sentences.

## Enumerating findings

Findings are enumerated rather than run together. Two forms predominate: numbered
items - (1) ... (2) ... (3) - appearing about 42 times across the corpus, and
"namely" followed by a list. A conclusion commonly opens by restating the aim,
then enumerates what was confirmed.

## Uncertainty and limitations

- Hedging is measured but present, around 3.8 instances per 1,000 words of *may*,
  *might*, *could*, *appears* and *likely*. It is specific hedging - naming what
  is uncertain - rather than blanket qualification.
- Observational findings take association verbs: "were associated with",
  "suggested". Causal verbs are reserved for designs that support them.
- Limitations get a dedicated paragraph, opened with "First, ... Second, ... In
  addition, ..." or "Primarily, ...".
- A limitation is often followed immediately by the reason it is tolerable, or by
  the direction of the bias: "However, as one of the most developed cities in
  China, it is quite comparable to ..."; "The adoption of waitlist control group
  may have resulted in inflated effect sizes."

Worth noting against convention: phrases such as "clinical implications" and
"should be interpreted with caution" are nearly absent from the corpus, and
"limitations" appears only about once per paper. The dedicated paragraph carries
the work without those markers.

## Numbers

- Sample sizes stated exactly and early: "a total of 59 informal caregivers
  expressed their interest", "N = 502".
- Percentages to one decimal where they are a share of a sample.
- Confidence intervals reported alongside estimates.
- Effect sizes interpreted against a stated threshold, and the threshold declared
  in the methods: "outcomes with effect sizes >= 0.3 were considered clinically
  meaningful".
- Numbers never rounded in a direction that flatters the result.

## Mechanics

- US spelling with -ize is dominant, roughly twice as frequent as -ise
  (*generalized* against *generalised*). Both appear in the corpus, which points
  to journal copyediting rather than a settled preference. Within one manuscript,
  pick one and keep it; a mixed paper reads as unedited.
- Hyphenated compound modifiers: "semi-structured", "audio-recorded",
  "culture-specific", "socio-demographic", "person-centered", "mixed-method".
- Instruments named in full at first use with the abbreviation following, then
  the abbreviation used consistently (PWD, CES-D, AD).
- Scale and category names in single quotes where the source uses them:
  'weak-not-sick', 'social distance'.

## Absences worth preserving

- No promotional adjectives. Nothing is "novel", "groundbreaking" or
  "game-changing".
- No causal language on associational evidence.
- No claim of generalisability without the sample's limits attached.
- No sentence that hedges into saying nothing.

## Writing for the author in this workspace

Separate from the published voice, and it governs how work is presented to them
rather than how a manuscript is written:

- Tables for anything with repeated structure across cases.
- The caveat sits next to the claim it qualifies, not in a closing paragraph.
- Facts, inferences and assumptions are labelled as such.
- Technical terms get a plain-language gloss in the same breath; the reader is
  often judging a claim outside their own specialism.
- No praise, no filler, no restating the question back.
- Say when something is unknown or could not be checked.
