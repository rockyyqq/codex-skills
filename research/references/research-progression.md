# Research progression

How a body of work moves from single-site studies to leading a large
collaborative trial, and how to generate questions worth that investment.

Read this when the request is about what to study next, building a research
programme, preparing a grant, or moving toward a multicentre trial. It is not
needed for an ordinary literature or citation task.

## The ladder, and where people stall

| Rung | Design | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Descriptive, qualitative, observational | The problem exists, and which target matters |
| 2 | Uncontrolled pilot or pre-post | Acceptability, and that delivery is possible |
| 3 | Pilot RCT with a control arm and pre-specified progression criteria | Feasibility: recruitment, retention, adherence, outcome completion |
| 4 | Single-site definitive RCT | Whether the intervention works |
| 5 | Multicentre collaborative trial with embedded economics and process evaluation | Whether it works at scale, for whom, at what cost |

The stall is almost always between 2 and 3, and the reason is diagnostic. An
uncontrolled pre-post study shows change but cannot separate the intervention
from regression to the mean, natural recovery, expectancy, or the attention that
comes with being in a study. A funder reads it as evidence of acceptability. It
cannot carry a causal claim, however large the within-group improvement.

## Progression criteria, and the sizing error

A feasibility trial is designed for feasibility outcomes, not for an effect
estimate: recruitment rate against target, retention, adherence, and outcome
completion. The stop, amend and go thresholds are set **before** recruitment
opens. A feasibility trial reported without pre-specified criteria has answered
the question only in hindsight.

The error that sinks applications: sizing the definitive trial from the effect
observed in the pilot. Pilot effects are imprecise and typically inflated, so a
trial sized that way is underpowered by construction. Size the definitive trial
from the smallest effect that would change practice. `sample-size-and-power.md`
covers how that number is arrived at, what has to be inflated for attrition and
clustering, and why the sensitivity range is reported rather than a single n.

Report the pilot against the CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility trials,
and register it before recruiting. Both are checkable, and their absence is
read as inexperience.

Multiplicity is worth getting ahead of. Eighteen significance tests in a pilot of
fifteen will attract the question. If twelve are significant against roughly one
expected by chance, that is reassuring rather than damning - say it in those
terms, and state that the definitive trial will have a single pre-specified
primary outcome.

## The comparator rung

A pilot RCT against a waitlist sits at the bottom of controlled designs. It
answers "better than waiting", which is almost never the decision a health system
faces, and it inflates the effect: waiting participants know they are waiting, and
their outcomes often deteriorate. A paper that reports a waitlist-controlled
pilot should say so and name the direction of that bias, because a reviewer will.

The next rung is an active comparator, and it is a real rung rather than a
formality. The sequence is waitlist, then treatment as usual, then the current
best alternative. Each step raises the bar and lowers the apparent effect, which
is the point: a definitive trial whose comparator is a waitlist invites the
criticism that the comparison was chosen to produce a result.

Two consequences for planning:

- Size the definitive trial against the effect that is plausible *opposite the
  intended comparator*, not the effect the pilot produced opposite a waitlist.
- Pick a primary outcome that matters to the decision the funder would make, at a
  single pre-specified timepoint. Where a pilot's effect is described as
  clinically meaningful only above a stated threshold, that threshold and its
  justification belong in the definitive trial's protocol.

Where a pilot has already been registered and reported, cite it as the feasibility
evidence, state explicitly which progression criteria it met, and quote its own
stated limitations rather than waiting to be asked about them. A funder reading
"effect sizes from such pilot studies might be unstable" in the applicant's own
earlier paper is reading a team that knows what it is doing.

## Generating questions worth the investment

Creativity in trial design is mostly recombination, and it is judgeable. Five
sources that produce fundable questions rather than merely interesting ones:

**A mechanism you have already shown.** The strongest position available: a
hypothesised mechanism with pilot evidence that modifying it changes the
outcome. Design the definitive trial to measure the mediator, not only the
outcome, so a null result still tells you something. Where a pilot moved exactly
the skills the mechanism predicts, and the items that sit outside the model did
not move, the pattern is coherent - and coherence is what a grant panel is
looking for.

**A problem the people delivering care cannot solve.** Source questions from
practitioners and from caregivers rather than from the literature's own gaps.
The literature's gaps are shaped by what has been funded before.

**A scale-up question.** "It works when delivered by a research team" becomes
"does it work when delivered by the staff who would deliver it in practice, at
the caseload they actually carry."

**A delivery-mode question.** Self-guided against guided, group against
individual, in-person against remote. These matter for reach, and they have
policy answers.

**An equity question.** Where every sample so far shares one background, whether
the effect holds across groups is a real question - and it is also a measurement
question, since comparing means across groups requires invariance (see
`measurement.md`).

Embedding an economic evaluation is what converts a clinical finding into a
commissioning decision, and it is the natural bridge from a health services
question to a cost-effectiveness answer.

### Filter before committing

Four questions. A no on any one is a reason not to proceed, not a reason to try
harder.

1. Can I recruit this population, at this rate, in this setting?
2. Is the comparator the current best alternative, and defensible to a reviewer?
3. Can the intervention be delivered at scale with measurable fidelity?
4. If the result is what I expect, will it change practice?

## What a large collaborative trial demands

The science is a minority of the work. A credible application also has:

- **A team, not a person.** Co-applicants across sites; a trial manager employed
  as such rather than a postdoc carrying it part time; an independent
  statistician; a health economist; a process evaluation or qualitative lead;
  data management; and patient and public involvement.
- **A sponsor** willing to sponsor, with the trial infrastructure that means.
- **Governance**: a trial steering committee, a data monitoring and ethics
  committee, and an independent oversight structure.
- **Regulatory and ethics**: institutional review board approval, GCP training,
  indemnity, and any applicable device or product regulation.
- **Site-level recruitment evidence.** Funders weight this heavily. Site numbers
  from a feasibility study are the currency.
- **A statistical analysis plan written before unblinding**, and a data
  management plan.
- **One primary outcome at one timepoint**, and a comparator justified against
  current practice.

The competencies this requires - protocol and analysis plan writing, budgeting,
trial management, regulatory navigation, and leading people who do not report to
you - are acquired rather than innate. The usual route is to hold them as a work
package lead on someone else's trial first.

## A realistic sequence

1. Join a multicentre trial as a work package lead. Process evaluation,
   qualitative study, or the economic evaluation are the standard entry points,
   and they use methods already in hand.
2. Lead a pilot RCT with pre-specified progression criteria.
3. Lead a single-site definitive trial, or an external pilot with progression to
   a multicentre design.
4. Lead a multicentre application, with a named senior mentor on the first one.

Each step produces the deliverable the next one is judged on. Skipping a rung is
usually visible in the application, because the evidence the funder wants is
precisely the thing the skipped rung would have produced.

## Funding routes

Singapore's national research funding runs schemes at different career stages and
scales, from individual grants for early-career investigators through to larger
collaborative and open-fund schemes. Adjacent routes include ministry health
services research funding, hospital and academic centre seed funding, and
disease-specific foundations and philanthropic funders.

Two rules, and the second matters more than the first:

- Never plan against a deadline, ceiling or eligibility rule you have not read
  in the current call document. These change annually, and a plan built on a
  remembered figure is a plan built on an assumption.
- Read the scheme's stated assessment criteria, then map your evidence onto them
  item by item. What is missing from that mapping is the work to do next, and it
  is a better guide than any general advice about career progression.

## Reviewing your own trajectory

At six to twelve month intervals, answer these in writing:

- Which rung am I on, and what evidence put me there?
- What is the single next thing that unlocks the rung above?
- What do I not yet have - a control arm, a statistician, a delivery site, a
  sponsor, a mentor?
- What would a funder need to see that I do not have, and what is the cheapest
  way to produce it?
- Which of my current projects produces a deliverable rather than another
  publication?

The last question is the uncomfortable one. Publication count is not the
currency of trial leadership; delivery is. A trial completed to target, on time,
with the recruitment it promised, is worth more to a future application than
several additional secondary analyses - and panels read a track record of
delivery as the strongest predictor of the next one.
