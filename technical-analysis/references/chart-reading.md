# Chart reading

Structure first. Indicators are transformations of the same prices and add nothing until
the structure is established.

## Trend

A trend is a sequence of higher highs and higher lows, or lower highs and lower lows. Say
which, over what period, and where the sequence would break.

- **Uptrend** - each rally exceeds the last high, each decline stops above the last low.
- **Downtrend** - the mirror.
- **Range** - neither, and the boundaries are the trade.

The most useful statement about a trend is not its direction but the price at which it is
no longer a trend. A sequence of higher lows on a daily chart is a fact; the level of the
most recent low is the invalidation.

Trends persist more often than they reverse, which is the empirical basis for
trend-following. That is a statement about the distribution of outcomes, not a prediction
about this one.

## Support and resistance

Levels matter because orders cluster there, not because lines have power. Identify them
from:

- prior swing highs and lows, especially where the price reversed decisively
- high-volume levels, where a lot of stock changed hands and positions were established
- round numbers, where resting orders accumulate
- the prior day's and prior week's close and range, for short-horizon work

**A level is a zone, not a line.** Say the zone. A "support at 78" that means 77.50 to
78.50 is honest; one that means exactly 78.00 will appear to be violated constantly.

**Support that has been tested repeatedly is weaker, not stronger.** Each test consumes
the resting orders. A level that has held four times has fewer buyers left beneath it than
one that has held once.

**When a level breaks, its role often reverses.** Prior support becomes resistance, for
the mechanical reason that everyone who bought there is now losing and sells into any
rally back to it.

## Volume

Volume is the only independent variable in the chart. Price shows what happened; volume
shows how much conviction was behind it.

- A breakout on volume well above average is more likely to hold than one on thin volume.
- A rally on declining volume is participants losing interest.
- A sharp fall on very heavy volume often marks a capitulation, which frequently - not
  always - precedes a bounce.

Compare volume to its own 20-day or 50-day average and report the ratio. "Twice average" is
a statement; "high volume" is not.

## Chart patterns

Patterns fall into three groups, and the groups are not equally reliable.

**Continuation patterns** - flags, pennants, ascending triangles. A pause within a trend.
Their logic is that the trend resumes, which is the same bet as trend-following and no more
reliable than it.

**Reversal patterns** - head and shoulders, double top and bottom, rounding tops. These
require a completed shape and a neckline break to be identified at all, by which time much
of the move has happened. Identifying one while it is still forming is a judgement with a
high error rate.

**Breakouts from ranges** - the most useful, because the level is unambiguous. The range
boundary is a fact; whether the break holds is not.

**Report the measured move as a convention, not a forecast.** Head-and-shoulders targets
and flagpole projections are conventions that practitioners use to set objectives. They
have no mechanism behind them. If you quote one, say that.

## Multiple timeframes

Declare the timeframe, because the same chart gives opposite answers at different scales.
A daily uptrend inside a weekly downtrend is a rally to sell or a pullback to buy depending
on which you are trading.

The workable convention: establish the trend on the timeframe you are trading, then use one
timeframe lower to time the entry, and one higher to check that you are not fighting
something much larger. Say which is which in the output.

## What the chart does not tell you

- **Why.** Price records transactions, not causes.
- **What participants intend.** Positioning data exists for futures and is published with a
  lag; everything else is inference.
- **Whether a pattern will resolve.** Base rates vary by market, period and definition, and
  most published pattern statistics are drawn from the same data that generated them.

An analysis that describes structure, levels, volume and the invalidation is useful. One
that names a pattern and implies a direction is doing something else.
