# Valuation spreads

Section 6 of the review. A measurement, kept separate from the price reading.

## The arithmetic

**Earnings yield = 1 ÷ P/E.** It is the earnings the price buys, expressed as a
rate, which is the only form that can be compared with a cash rate.

**Spread = earnings yield − hurdle rate.** Positive means the investment earns
more than the safe alternative; negative means it is priced *below* cash.

**Dividend yield** is the cash actually received. It is lower than the earnings
yield by the retained portion, and it is the figure that matters for an income
holding.

## Choosing the hurdle rate

**Name it, and say why.** Every spread depends on this choice, and the same
security can clear one hurdle and fail another.

| Candidate | When it is the right hurdle |
|---|---|
| **Local government 10-year** | The default for a long-horizon equity comparison |
| **Deposit or savings rate actually earned** | The most honest hurdle for money that would otherwise sit in that account |
| **Retirement-account rate** | Where the alternative really is the CPF or equivalent |
| **A bond you can actually buy** | Better than a theoretical rate |

**State which one you used and what a different choice would do.** A spread of
+3.3 against a 5.0% hurdle becomes +6.3 against a 2.0% one — and at a 2.0%
hurdle almost everything looks cheap, which is why the choice must be visible.

| The broad market | | | | |

**Include what the household already holds.** If an existing index fund gives
the same exposure at a wider spread, that is the finding — and it has ended
several reviews in one step.

**Peers are fetched, never recalled.** A multiple quoted from memory is not
evidence.

## What a positive spread does and does not mean

**It does mean** the price is defensible against the safe alternative, and the
holder is being paid something for the risk.

**It does not mean** the price will go up. A spread is a statement about today's
relative pricing, not a forecast. **And it does not address the price history** —
which is why this section stays separate from the chart reading.

**A high yield is not automatically a warning and not automatically a bargain.**
Where a payout ratio is high against earnings, the question that decides it is
whether it is covered by free cash flow. **If statements are not reachable, say
the cover is unchecked** rather than implying it from the payout.

## A worked comparison

Against a 5.00% hurdle, from one week's reviews:

| | P/E | Earnings yield | Spread |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A dividend index the household holds | 8.97 | **11.15%** | **+6.15** |
| A peer telecom | 9.7 | 10.31% | +5.31 |
| A large telecom | 11.3 | 8.85% | +3.85 |
| A smaller telecom | 12.0 | 8.32% | +3.32 |
| The broad market index | 13.36 | 7.49% | +2.49 |
| A single carmaker | 21.2 | 4.72% | −0.28 |
| A technology index | 43.31 | 2.31% | −2.69 |
| A semiconductor index | 73.03 | 1.37% | −3.63 |

**The ranking inverted the intuitive answer.** The index the household already
held carried the widest spread; two "growth" indices were priced below cash; and
a five-year return chart would have ranked them in almost the opposite order.

**That is why this section exists and why it is separate from the price
reading.** The chart describes where prices have been. Only valuation says what
they are worth against the alternative.
